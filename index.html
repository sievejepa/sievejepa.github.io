SIEVE

Recover each factor's block of a self-supervised embedding by pairing states, differencing their moments, and taking eigen-directions.

Technical write-up Code: coming soon

The method in 20 seconds, on a synthetic world (no video): 5 hidden factors with their own rhythms, a 24-D embedding, SIEVE run rung by rung without whitening. Read-outs and scores come from that run; coloured curves are linearly scaled to the true factor for display.

What it does

A frozen encoder maps observations to an embedding. The world behind the observations is made of several factors (for example cube position, colour, lighting), each spanning several dimensions. SIEVE finds, for each factor, the subspace of the embedding that carries it. It does not recover single coordinates: a multi-dimensional factor has no privileged axes, so the target is the subspace up to a rotation inside it.

Factor
One independent source of variation in the world; here a block of q = 4 latent dimensions.
Block
The q embedding directions SIEVE assigns to one factor.
Rung
One step of the ladder; rung k recovers factor k.
Own-factor R²
How well a linear probe on the block predicts its own factor.
Ceiling
The same probe's R² using the full embedding; scores below are own-factor R² divided by this.

How it works

1. Pairing is an intervention

Take two states that share some factors and redraw the others independently. The difference of their embeddings then depends only on the redrawn factors. Rung k redraws one more factor than rung k−1, so the pair distributions are nested.

2. The moment of the differences

For each rung, form the uncentred second moment of the pair differences:

M_k = (1/N) · Σ_pairs  d dᵀ ,   d = h(x_a) − h(x_b)

It is deliberately not a covariance. Subtract the previous rung:

ΔM_k = M_k − M_(k−1)

ΔM_k contains only what factor k adds. Its spectrum is kept signed: with finite samples some eigenvalues come out negative, and they are reported, never clipped. In the animation above they appear in red below the axis; by rung 16 about half of the 68 eigenvalues are negative, all tiny.

3. Eigen-directions

The top-q eigenvectors (largest positive eigenvalues) of ΔM_k form block k. The ladder stops at the first rung whose positive rank is below q, and it never pads.

4. Whitening, and its floor

Whitening rescales the embedding so every direction has unit variance, using 1/sqrt(max(λ, eps)) on the covariance eigenvalues λ. Directions with λ below eps are divided by √eps instead of their own scale, so they stay under-scaled. This floor, not the idea of whitening, is what limited the high-condition-number runs below.

  • Without whitening, recovery falls steadily as axis scales spread. A candidate cause is that a small-scale factor's moment eigenvalues are outranked by nonlinear residue on large-scale axes; this explanation has not been tested separately.
  • With whitening, the drop at 1e8 comes from the eigenvalue floor: 26 of 68 eigenvalues are below eps = 1e-5. Lowering eps to 1e-8 restores 0.997.
  • Per-axis standardisation and a generalised eigenproblem (solve ΔM r = λ C r with C the embedding covariance, by Cholesky, no floor) also restore it. The rescaling here is exactly diagonal, so per-axis scaling is a favourable fit.

Within a block, SIEVE's eigenvectors are an arbitrary rotation. A separate experiment that rotated each block by a sparsity criterion (a DSReg-style footprint penalty) raised single-coordinate recovery from 0.61 to 0.96 of the ceiling on the same synthetic setup, on a favourable near-identity map.

What these results do not show. The synthetic world is favourable: a near-linear, invertible encoder, independent Gaussian latents and exactly matched pairs. Scores near 0.999 are expected there. On the PickCube autoencoder, recovery was only partial, and full whitening spread each factor over roughly 870–1,400 embedding directions against 2–11 without whitening. Why whitening helps in one setting and hurts in the other is untested. Negative controls (shuffled pairs, random subspaces) are being run.
