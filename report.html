SIEVE: pulling factors out of an encoder, one at a time

A short report. The numbers are kept for the details page.

The question

Take a picture of a robot scene. A few things can change in it: where the cube is, its colour, the light, the camera angle. An encoder turns the picture into a long list of numbers.

Somewhere in that list, each of those things is stored. Can we find out where, so that we get one small piece of the list per thing?

The idea

Take two pictures that are the same except for one thing, say the cube moved. Look at how the list changed. Only the cube's part of the list moved. That tells us where the cube lives.

Now take two pictures where the cube and the colour are different. More of the list moves. The new part, compared with before, belongs to the colour.

So we climb a ladder. Each step lets one more thing differ. Whatever is new at a step belongs to the thing we just added. We take that piece out and go on to the next step. Like a sieve: each pass catches one kind of thing.

At each step the moving part gets bigger. The new bit is the piece we keep.

Test 1: a clean made-up world

First we built a world where we know the answer. The factors are smooth random numbers (Gaussian), and the encoder is trained to undo the mixing.

Result: every factor came back almost perfectly, in a piece of exactly the right size, with nothing leaking from one factor into another. When the encoder keeps the information, the method finds it.

Test 2: robot pictures

Then we tried a simulated robot arm picking up a cube. Nine things vary: gripper opening, arm pose, cube yaw, cube position, cube colour, light tint, brightness and the camera. We used an autoencoder and asked how much of each factor we could get back.

The pattern is simple. If the encoder stores a factor strongly, SIEVE recovers it strongly. If the encoder barely stores it, there is little to recover. This held both with and without whitening (a standard step that evens out the list before we start).

What whitening changes

With whitening, each factor comes out spread over hundreds of directions in the list. Without it, the same factor sits in just a few. The factor is found either way, but without whitening its piece is much smaller and tidier.

Why does it spread? Our reading: the autoencoder is non-linear and the factors come in steps (a few levels each), while the clean world was linear and Gaussian. We have not tested this directly. It is our best explanation, not a result.

What this means

SIEVE finds what is in the encoder. If a factor is stored strongly, it comes out. If the encoder lost it on the way in, no method could get it back. So when recovery is weak, look at the encoder, not the method.
